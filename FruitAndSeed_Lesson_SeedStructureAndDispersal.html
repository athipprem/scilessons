Quick Recall from Lesson 1 🔁

🔁 Remember? After fertilization, the OVARY WALL develops into the FRUIT, and each OVULE develops into a SEED. The fruit-or-vegetable rule has nothing to do with taste — it's only about whether a plant part grew from a fertilized ovary and holds seeds.

So a seed is already sitting inside its fruit, waiting. What's actually packed inside that seed — and what job does the fruit do for it next?

Why learn this? 🤔

You already know a seed needs water, warmth, and air to germinate (Unit 25) — but a seed can only use those things if it's carrying its own tiny food supply and protection to survive the wait. And a plant can't just walk to a new spot — so understanding how its fruit gets the seed there explains why fruits look so different from each other.
Real life: a squirrel burying an acorn (helping an oak tree's seed travel and get planted); a burr sticking to your sock after a walk (the plant's fruit hitching a ride); the fluffy white parachute on a dandelion seed drifting on the wind.

Inside a Seed 🌰

Every seed has three parts: a SEED COAT (protection), a FOOD STORE (energy), and an EMBRYO (the baby plant itself).
The seed coat (brown, outside) protects everything inside. The food store (cream) is packed energy. The embryo (green, centre) is the tiny baby plant that will grow roots and a shoot once it germinates.
Key idea: the embryo can't make its own food yet — no leaves, no sunlight reaching it inside the dark seed coat — so the food store is what powers germination until the first real leaves open up and photosynthesis can take over.

The Fruit's Job: Helping the Seed Travel 🚀

A plant can't uproot itself and walk somewhere better. So instead, many plants build their fruit to do the traveling for the seed — and the fruit's shape is a big clue to HOW it travels.

🍇
Animal (eaten)
juicy, sweet, colorful fruit gets eaten; seeds pass through and are dropped elsewhere
🌰
Animal (carried/hooked)
sticky hooks or a tasty shell get carried or buried by an animal
🌬️
Wind
light, fluffy, or wing-shaped fruit catches a breeze
💥
Mechanical (pops open)
a dry pod dries out, splits, and flings seeds outward
Watch out: not every fruit is juicy and sweet — a dry, brown pea pod is just as much a real fruit as a juicy plum. The fruit's job (protect + help travel) matters more than what it looks or tastes like.

Trap-Recognition Drill #3 🚨

Watch for words like ALWAYS, NEVER, ONLY, ALL, and EVERY in claims about fruits and seeds — a real exception is almost always hiding somewhere.

Trap example: "A fruit is always sweet and eaten as a dessert."
Why it's false: tomatoes, cucumbers, and pea pods are all real fruits, and none of them are sweet desserts.

Trap example: "All seeds need soil to germinate."
Why it's false: from Unit 25 you already know a seed needs water, air, and the right warmth to germinate — soil itself isn't on that list; seeds can even germinate on wet paper towel.

Quick check: "Every fruit is round in shape." Is this trustworthy?

✔ Correct — "every" is a strong claim, and plenty of real fruits (pods, bananas) aren't round at all.
✘ Re-think it — the word "every" should make you look for an exception. Pea pods and bananas are fruits that aren't round.

Try it: a coconut floats on ocean currents and washes up on distant beaches — not juicy-and-eaten, not hooked, not fluffy or light. It doesn't fit any of those branches neatly, which is a clue that WATER is actually a fifth dispersal method for some fruits, alongside these four.

Worked Example: Dandelion vs Burr 🌾

Set-up: A dandelion seed has a tiny fluffy white parachute attached. A burr seed case is covered in tiny hooks. Both formed from a flower's ovary-and-ovule pathway, exactly like every other fruit and seed.

Wrong explanation (a trap!): "Their shapes are just random — plants don't design their fruit on purpose." This ignores that a fruit's traits closely match a specific dispersal method, which is far too consistent to be random.
Correct explanation: the dandelion's fluffy parachute is light enough to be carried by even a gentle breeze — a WIND-dispersed design. The burr's hooks are built to catch onto fur or clothing as an animal brushes past — an ANIMAL-CARRIED design. Same starting pathway (ovary → fruit, ovule → seed), but two completely different fruit shapes because they evolved to match two different journeys.
🧠 Habit to build: when you see an unfamiliar fruit or seed, look at its shape and surface first — light and fluffy, hooked and sticky, juicy and colorful, or dry and cracked open — and match it to a dispersal method before answering.
