Quick Recall from Unit 1 🔁

🔁 Remember Unit 1? Every plant has the SAME three jobs to do: roots ANCHOR the plant and DRINK water from the soil; the stem SUPPORTS the plant and TRANSPORTS water and food; leaves catch sunlight to MAKE FOOD (photosynthesis).

Today we ask a new question: what happens to those jobs when a plant lives somewhere really extreme — like a bone-dry desert, or floating right on top of a pond? The jobs stay the same, but HOW each part does its job changes completely.

Why learn this? 🤔

Farmers and gardeners choose plants based on climate — a cactus would rot in a swamp, and a water lily would shrivel up in a desert. ASMOPSS loves asking "why does this plant have this feature?" questions — understanding the LINK between a habitat and a feature is exactly the kind of applied thinking the exam rewards, far more than just memorising plant names.
Real life: people who landscape gardens in hot, dry climates deliberately choose desert plants (succulents, cacti) because they need very little watering; people with garden ponds choose floating plants like water lilies because they know normal soil-rooted plants would simply drown.

Desert Plants: Surviving on Almost No Water 🌵

A cactus's whole body is built around ONE problem: how do you survive for months without rain?
Every part of a cactus solves the "almost no water" problem in its own way.
Spines instead of big leaves: a cactus's leaves are shrunk down into thin, hard spines. Less leaf surface means less water lost through evaporation — and the spines also stop thirsty animals from eating the plant.
A thick, fleshy stem: since the leaves barely do any photosynthesis anymore, the STEM takes over — it's green and does photosynthesis itself, and its thick, spongy inside stores huge amounts of water for the dry months ahead.
Wide, shallow roots: instead of digging deep, cactus roots spread out very wide, just under the surface — so when rain finally does fall, even a light shower, the roots can soak up as much of it as possible before it evaporates or sinks too deep to reach.

Water Plants: Floating Free 🪷

A water plant like duckweed has the OPPOSITE problem to a cactus: it has more water than it could ever need — so its whole body is built completely differently.
Duckweed is one of the smallest flowering plants in the world, and it floats completely freely on ponds and slow rivers.
No need to anchor: a normal plant's roots grip the soil to stop the plant falling over — but duckweed just floats, held up by the water itself. Its roots are short and thin, and they dangle loosely without ever touching the bottom.
Drinking is now the ONLY root job: since the water is doing the anchoring job for free, duckweed's roots have really just ONE job left — soaking up water and minerals directly from all the water around them, which is so easy that the roots barely need to be strong at all.
Floating leaves: duckweed's tiny round leaves float flat on the surface, catching sunlight from above, with air pockets inside that help keep the whole plant bobbing at the surface instead of sinking.

Adaptation Detective 🔍

Scientists can often guess a plant's habitat just by looking at its features — that's exactly the kind of reasoning ASMOPSS tests.

Clue set A: thick, fleshy, water-storing stem; spines instead of leaves; wide, shallow, spread-out roots.
Clue set B: tiny flat leaves that float; short dangling roots that never reach the bottom; no thick water-storing stem at all.

What habitat does each set point to? Clue set A = a plant built to survive long dry spells (desert). Clue set B = a plant that already has unlimited water and never needs to anchor itself (floating on water).

The detective trick: ask "what problem would this feature solve?" A thick water-storing stem solves "not enough water." Short, weak, dangling roots only make sense if something else (the water) is already doing the anchoring job.

Habitat Decision Tree 🌳

Does the plant have a thick, water-storing stem and small spines instead of big leaves?
Yes — built to survive long dry spells
DESERT PLANT
No — instead has short, weak roots that never touch the bottom, and floats on the surface
WATER PLANT
How to use this: look for the water-storage clue first (a thick, fleshy stem plus spines strongly points to desert). If that's missing, but the roots are short, weak, and don't anchor to anything solid, that's the water-plant clue instead.

1. Why does a cactus have spines instead of big, flat leaves?

✔ Correct — less leaf surface area means less water lost through evaporation, which matters enormously in a desert.
✘ Re-check Screen 4 — spines are shrunk-down leaves, and less leaf surface means less water lost.

2. Why doesn't duckweed need strong roots that grip the bottom of the pond?

✔ Correct — since duckweed floats and is supported by the water, its roots only need to absorb water and minerals, not grip anything.
✘ Re-check Screen 5 — the water supports the whole plant, so the roots' anchoring job disappears entirely.

3. In a desert plant, which part usually takes over the leaf's job of making food through photosynthesis?

✔ Correct — the cactus's green, fleshy stem photosynthesizes since the leaves have shrunk into spines.
✘ Re-check Screen 4 — with leaves reduced to spines, the green stem takes over food-making.

4. Why do cactus roots spread out wide and shallow instead of growing deep?

✔ Correct — desert rain is rare and often light, so spreading wide near the surface lets the roots grab it quickly.
✘ Re-check Screen 4 — spreading wide and shallow is about catching rare rainfall fast, before it disappears.

5. A student finds a plant with tiny floating leaves and short, weak roots dangling loosely in the water, never touching the bottom. What habitat is this plant almost certainly adapted to?

✔ Correct — floating leaves and short, non-anchoring roots are the classic water-plant combination, just like duckweed.
✘ Re-check Screen 6 — short, weak, non-gripping roots only make sense if the water itself is already holding the plant up.
