Quick Recall from Unit 9 & Unit 2 🔁

🔁 Remember Unit 9 (Chemical Reactions)? When a chemical reaction happens, the atoms rearrange into brand-new substances with different properties — you can't just cool the ash back into wood, the same way you can't un-bake a cake. 🔁 Remember Unit 2 (What Is Matter)? Air is a mixture of gases, not one single gas — it's mostly nitrogen, with a smaller share of oxygen, plus tiny amounts of other gases.

Put those two ideas together and burning makes perfect sense: it's a chemical reaction where a fuel reacts with ONE specific gas from that air mixture — not "the air" in general.

Why learn this? 🤔

Fire is everywhere in daily life — cooking stoves, campfires, birthday candles, car engines, even the Sun. Understanding exactly what a fire needs is also exactly how firefighters know how to put fires OUT, and how safety rules at home and school are designed. Get the fire triangle solid, and both the science AND the safety side make sense together.
Real life: a chef turning off a gas stove's flame the instant the gas valve is shut (fuel removed); a firefighter spraying water on a burning house (heat removed); a candle snuffer cutting off air to a candle flame (oxygen removed).

What Is Combustion? 🔥

Combustion is the scientific name for burning — a chemical reaction where a fuel (something that can burn, like wood, gas, or wax) reacts quickly with oxygen from the air, releasing heat and light and forming new substances.

What actually happens when wood burns: the wood (fuel) combines with oxygen from the air. New substances form — mostly carbon dioxide gas and water vapour (which float away, so you don't see them), plus the ash and soot left behind. This is exactly like Unit 9's chemical reactions: new substances form, and you can't turn the ash back into wood by cooling it down.

The Fire Triangle 🔺

A fire needs all THREE sides of the triangle at once. Take away just ONE side, and the fire goes out.
The fire triangle: FUEL (something that can burn), OXYGEN (from the air), and HEAT (enough to start and keep the reaction going). All three corners must be present at the same time.
Key rule: remove ANY ONE side and the fire stops — no fuel left, no oxygen reaching it, or cooled below its ignition temperature. This single rule explains almost every fire-safety method that exists.

Oxygen, Not Nitrogen 💨

Air is roughly 78% nitrogen and 21% oxygen. Nitrogen is unreactive — it does NOT support burning. Oxygen is the part of the air that a fire actually uses up.

It's tempting to think "nitrogen must matter, since there's so much more of it in the air" — but being the MOST COMMON gas doesn't mean it's the one doing the job. Nitrogen mostly just sits there, chemically unreactive, while the much smaller share of oxygen is what combines with the fuel.

Proof: if you could somehow burn something in pure nitrogen with no oxygen at all, it would NOT catch fire, no matter how much you tried to light it. But burning something in extra pure oxygen makes it burn much more fiercely and quickly than in normal air.

Watch out: "there's more of it, so it must be doing more" is a trap. Being common and being reactive are two completely different things — always check what a gas actually DOES, not just how much of it is there.

Worked Example: The Candle Under a Jar 🕯️

Set-up: A lit candle is covered with a large glass jar, sealing it off from the room's air. After about a minute, the flame shrinks and goes out — even though the candle still has plenty of wax left, and the jar hasn't gotten any colder.

Wrong explanation (a trap!): "The wax must have run out." This is a classic overgeneralized-sounding claim that doesn't fit the evidence — there's clearly still wax left.
Correct explanation: the sealed jar only had a limited amount of oxygen inside it to begin with. As the flame burned, it used up that oxygen and turned it into carbon dioxide — and since the jar is sealed, no fresh oxygen from the room could get in to replace it. Once the oxygen level inside dropped too low, the OXYGEN corner of the triangle failed, and the flame went out — with fuel (wax) still left over.
🧠 Habit to build: whenever a "why did it stop" question comes up, check ALL THREE triangle sides against the evidence before picking an answer — don't just grab the first explanation that sounds familiar.
