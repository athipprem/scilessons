Quick Warm-Up 🔁

This is an UNGRADED refresher pulling from Unit 9 — reversible changes — content you haven't seen tested in a while. Just to keep it fresh.

Warm-up. Prem melts an ice cube into water, then puts the water in the freezer and it turns back into ice. Is melting ice a REVERSIBLE or IRREVERSIBLE change?

✔ Correct — melting and freezing are REVERSIBLE changes of state; the water is still the same substance, just in a different state.
✘ Re-think it: can the water be turned back into ice by cooling it again? If yes, that's the definition of reversible.

Warm-up. A piece of paper is burned to ash. Can the ash be changed back into paper?

✔ Correct — burning creates a brand-new substance (ash and smoke) that cannot be turned back into paper, so it's IRREVERSIBLE.
✘ Re-think it: has a completely new substance been formed here, one that didn't exist before the change?

Why learn this? 🤔

Your school book's Chapter 1 covered the Sun, Earth, Moon, the 8 planets, and satellites — but competition papers like ASMOPSS often go one step further, asking WHY things happen (like eclipses) rather than just naming what's out there. Quick Recap: The Sun & Earth ☀️🌍

From Unit 43: the Sun is a STAR — a giant ball of hot gases that makes its own light and heat. Earth has three layers (crust, mantle, core) and orbits the Sun once a year while spinning on its axis once a day. The Moon orbits Earth roughly every 27–29 days, reflecting the Sun's light rather than making its own.
Today we ask: what happens when the Sun, Earth, and Moon all line up in a straight line? And what invisible force keeps dangerous particles from the Sun away from us?

Solar Eclipse ☀️🌑

A SOLAR ECLIPSE happens when the MOON passes directly between the Sun and Earth, blocking the Sun's light from reaching a small part of Earth.
The Moon's shadow only covers a small part of Earth at a time — that's why a solar eclipse is only ever visible from a narrow path, not the whole planet at once.
Key idea: a solar eclipse can only happen at NEW MOON (when the Moon is between the Sun and Earth). It doesn't happen at every new moon, though — the Moon's orbit is slightly tilted, so most months its shadow misses Earth completely, passing just above or below.
Safety note: never look directly at the Sun during a solar eclipse without special eclipse glasses — even when most of it is covered, the small sliver still visible can seriously damage your eyes.

Lunar Eclipse 🌍🌕

A LUNAR ECLIPSE happens when the EARTH passes directly between the Sun and the Moon, blocking sunlight from reaching the Moon.
The Moon passes through Earth's shadow and can turn a dim reddish colour — sometimes called a "blood moon" — because a little sunlight still bends through Earth's atmosphere and reaches it.
Key idea: a lunar eclipse can only happen at FULL MOON (when Earth is between the Sun and the Moon). Unlike a solar eclipse, a lunar eclipse can be seen from the entire night-side of Earth at once, not just a narrow path — which is why lunar eclipses are seen by far more people than solar eclipses.

Solar or Lunar? 🔍

Which object sits in the MIDDLE of the straight line?
↓
The MOON is in the middle (blocking the Sun)
SOLAR ECLIPSE — happens at New Moon
The EARTH is in the middle (blocking sunlight to the Moon)

Key idea: eclipses are rare (not every month) because the Moon's orbit is tilted slightly compared to Earth's orbit around the Sun — the three objects only line up closely enough a few times a year.

Earth's Invisible Shield 🛡️

The Sun constantly streams out tiny charged particles called the SOLAR WIND. Earth has TWO shields that protect us from it: a magnetic field, and the atmosphere.
Key idea — the magnetic field: deep inside Earth's metal core, moving liquid metal creates a giant MAGNETIC FIELD that surrounds the whole planet, like an invisible force field. It deflects most of the solar wind's charged particles around Earth instead of letting them crash into us.
Key idea — the atmosphere: the layer of gases wrapped around Earth (which you also learned makes life possible, back in Unit 43) blocks most of the Sun's harmful radiation and burns up small space rocks before they reach the ground.
Bonus fact: near the North and South Poles, some solar wind particles do slip through and collide with gases in the atmosphere, making them glow — this beautiful glow is called the AURORA (the Northern or Southern Lights).
