Quick Fix 🧫

This is an UNGRADED confirming check from Unit 44 — Cells. On the real test, two questions got mixed up between "how MANY cells a body has" and "how OFTEN cells are actively dividing right now." Let's check that's solid before moving on.

Quick Fix. An elephant is much BIGGER than a mouse, so an elephant's body is made of far more cells overall. Does this mean an elephant's cells are dividing for growth MORE OFTEN, right now, than a young mouse pup's cells?

✔ Correct — total cell NUMBER (body size) is not the same thing as division RATE right now. A young, still-growing animal has faster growth-division than a full-grown adult of a much bigger species.
✘ Re-think it: does having more cells overall tell you how fast NEW cells are being made this very moment, or just how big the body has already become?

Quick Fix. A puppy's baby teeth fall out and are replaced by new adult teeth as it grows. What purpose of cell division does making those NEW teeth match?

✔ Correct — building brand-new adult teeth as a growing puppy's body gets bigger is GROWTH-purpose cell division, not repair (repair replaces LOST or DAMAGED cells, not builds new permanent structures for the first time).
✘ Re-think it: is the puppy replacing something that was damaged, or building new structures because its whole body is still getting bigger?

Quick Warm-Up 🪐

This is an UNGRADED refresher pulling from Unit 21 — gravity and falling objects — content you haven't seen tested in a while. Just to keep it fresh.

Warm-up. You drop a bowling ball and a tennis ball from the same height at the same moment, and air resistance isn't strong enough to make a real difference. Which one hits the ground first?

✔ Correct — without air resistance making a real difference, gravity accelerates every object at the same rate, so mass alone doesn't decide who lands first.
✘ Re-think it: does gravity pull harder on a heavier object, or does it pull every object down at the same rate?

Warm-up. A flat sheet of paper falls much more slowly than the exact same paper crumpled into a tight ball. Why?

✔ Correct — a flat sheet has much more surface area facing the air than a crumpled ball, so air resistance pushes back on it harder and slows its fall more.
✘ Re-think it: the paper's weight hasn't changed at all — so what HAS changed between a flat sheet and a crumpled ball?

Recap: The Five Vertebrate Groups 🐾

From Unit 31: vertebrates (animals with a backbone) are sorted into five groups: mammals (fur/hair, e.g. dogs, elephants), birds (feathers, e.g. chickens, eagles), reptiles (dry scaly skin, e.g. lizards, turtles), amphibians (moist skin, live in water AND on land, e.g. frogs), and fish (scales, gills, live fully in water, e.g. guppies, salmon).
NEW idea: each of these five groups has its OWN typical way of reproducing — where fertilisation happens, and how the young grow up. That's exactly what the rest of this unit explores.

Sexual Reproduction: Male + Female 🐣

Animals reproduce SEXUALLY — this means TWO parents are needed: a MALE parent and a FEMALE parent. The male produces sperm; the female produces eggs.
FERTILISATION is when a sperm (from the male) joins together with an egg (from the female). This joining is what starts a brand-new life — the beginning of the offspring.
Key idea: without both a male parent's sperm AND a female parent's egg meeting, fertilisation cannot happen, and no new animal can begin growing. This is different from how some tiny microorganisms reproduce — remember from Unit 4 that a bacterium can simply divide itself in two, with no mate needed at all.

Where Does Fertilisation Happen? 🏠🌊

Fertilisation can happen in TWO different places: INSIDE the female's body (internal), or OUTSIDE the body (external, usually in water).
Internal fertilisation happens inside the female's body. External fertilisation happens outside the body — the egg and sperm are released into water, where they meet.
INTERNAL fertilisation: the sperm meets the egg INSIDE the female's body. Used by mammals, birds, and reptiles.
EXTERNAL fertilisation: the sperm meets the egg OUTSIDE the body, usually in water. Used by most fish and most amphibians.
Key idea: water-living or water-breeding animals (most fish, most amphibians) don't need their fertilisation to happen inside the body — the water itself carries the sperm to the egg. Land animals that mate on dry ground (mammals, birds, reptiles) need fertilisation to happen INSIDE, where it's protected and moist.

Internal or External? 🔍

Which vertebrate group is the animal in?
↓
Mammal, bird, or reptile
INTERNAL fertilisation — inside the female's body
Most fish or most amphibians
EXTERNAL fertilisation — usually in water
Key idea: knowing an animal's vertebrate group is usually a strong clue to which type of fertilisation it uses — but it's the ENVIRONMENT (does mating happen in water, or on land?) that's the real reason why, not the group label itself.
