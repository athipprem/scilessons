Quick Warm-Up 🔁

This is an UNGRADED refresher pulling from Unit 40 — mixtures, solutions & separating materials. It's also the exact chemistry idea behind today's Lesson 2 (osmosis is all about particles moving through tiny holes).

Warm-up. A bowl contains water with small stones mixed in, but nothing dissolved. Which separating method gets the stones out fastest?

✔ Correct — sieving is for solid pieces that are simply too big to pass through the holes, with nothing dissolved involved at all.
✘ Re-think it: are the stones DISSOLVED in the water, or just mixed in as solid pieces?

Warm-up (spot the flaw). A classmate claims: "Filtering ALWAYS removes everything that is dissolved in the water, no matter what it is." Is this claim correct?

✔ Correct — filtering only ever catches solid, undissolved bits. Once something has truly dissolved, it's part of the liquid and slips straight through the filter paper with the water.
✘ Re-think it: once sugar dissolves into water, can you still filter it back out as a separate solid?

Quick Fix from Unit 48 🪜

On your real Unit 48 test, three misses (Q3, Q12, Q13) shared one gap: you kept naming the SYSTEM a structure serves ("respiratory system," "digestive system") instead of the structure itself ("organ"). The ladder's ORDER wasn't the problem — the PLACEMENT decision was.
The rule, in words: if you can point at it as ONE single lump — one thing you could physically pick up — it is an ORGAN. If it's SEVERAL organs working together as a team, it is an ORGAN SYSTEM. The lungs are one lump → organ. The respiratory system is lungs + windpipe + diaphragm, all working together → system.
Today's fresh pathway — how a plant takes up water:
Root hair cells (specialised for soaking up water, from Unit 14) team up into root TISSUE, root tissue plus other tissues build THE ROOT (an organ), roots working together across the plant form the ROOT SYSTEM (an organ system), and every system together makes the WHOLE PLANT (the organism).
REP (a) · Box A: What IS it?

Look only at "the root" itself — one single root, on its own. What IS it, physically?

✔ Correct — one root, on its own, is ONE lump you could physically pull out of the soil. That makes it an ORGAN.
✘ Re-think it: can you point at ONE root as a single physical thing, or is it several separate organs working together?
REP (a) · Box B: What system does it belong to?

Now the second box — a DIFFERENT question. Which larger team does that one root belong to?

✔ Correct — that one root is a member of the ROOT SYSTEM, the team of every root on the plant working together. Notice Box A and Box B have DIFFERENT answers — "what is it" and "what team is it on" are two separate questions.
✘ Re-think it: which named team is built from roots specifically?
REP (b) · Box A: What IS it?

Now look at "the root SYSTEM" itself — every root on the plant, together. What IS it, physically?

✔ Correct — the root system is every root working together, so it's an ORGAN SYSTEM, not an organ.
✘ Re-think it: is "the root system" ONE root, or ALL the roots together?
REP (b) · Box B: Why is this a DIFFERENT answer from (a)?

Compared to (a) "the root" (an organ), why is "the root system" a different rung on the ladder?

✔ Correct — "the root" and "the root system" sound almost the same, but one is a single organ and the other is that organ's whole team, one rung higher.
✘ Re-think it: does "the root system" mean just one root, or every root added together?
REP (c) · Reverse: name the rung from a description

Here's a NEW description, not a name: long tube-like structures running the length of the stem, carrying water upward. What rung of the ladder is this?

✔ Correct — a group of the SAME kind of cell working together (here, water-carrying tube cells) is a TISSUE, one rung above a single cell.
✘ Re-think it: is this describing ONE cell, a group of the SAME kind of cell, or several different tissues working together?

What the Parts Are Made Of 🧱

NEW idea: your book names the plant-only parts. Here's what each one is actually MADE OF, and what job it really does.
Cell wall: made of a tough material called cellulose. It's rigid (stiff, doesn't bend easily), gives the cell its shape and support — and here's the surprising part: it's fully permeable. Everything passes straight through it, with nothing blocked at all.
Cell membrane: a thin, flexible layer that sits just inside the wall. Unlike the wall, the membrane actually controls what enters and leaves the cell.
Chloroplast: holds a green pigment called chlorophyll, which traps light energy. That's the very first step of photosynthesis (Unit 8) — but we won't restate that whole equation here, just where it starts.
Large central vacuole: a big, water-filled sac holding a liquid called cell sap. As it fills, it pushes outward against the wall, keeping the whole cell firm.

The Hinge Question 🤔

Think about it: the WALL lets absolutely everything pass straight through it — that's what "fully permeable" means. So if the wall isn't stopping anything… what is actually doing the choosing about what gets in and out of the cell?
Answer: the MEMBRANE. Most people assume the wall is the gatekeeper, since it's the outermost layer you'd meet first. But the wall lets everything straight through — it's the membrane, sitting just inside it, that actually decides.
Key idea: the outermost layer of something isn't always the part in charge. That single idea — that the real "chooser" is hidden one layer in, not sitting on the very outside — is exactly what Lesson 2 is built on.

Two Ways to See It — Route 1 🔗

Permeability is the one genuinely new idea today. Here it is TWO ways — pick whichever clicks for you, or use both.
Concrete route — a picture you can imagine

Picture a chain-link fence standing just outside a screen door. Anything at all can walk straight through the gaps in the chain-link fence — big or small, it doesn't stop a thing. But the screen door just behind it only lets air and tiny insects through; a person can't get past it. The fence is the WALL. The screen door is the MEMBRANE.

Two Ways to See It — Route 2 🔗

Same idea, now as a step-by-step rule instead of a picture.
Procedural route — the rule, one step at a time
  1. Step 1: What is the outer layer? → the cell wall.
  2. Step 2: Does it stop anything from passing through? → No — it's fully permeable.
  3. Step 3: What is the next layer in? → the cell membrane.
  4. Step 4: Does IT stop anything? → Yes — it only lets some things through.
  5. Step 5: Therefore, the real "chooser" is… → the INNER layer, the membrane, not the outer one.
Key idea: whichever route clicked for you — the fence-and-screen-door picture, or the five-step rule — they both land on the same answer: the membrane chooses, not the wall.

One More Question About the Wall 📦

Higher-tier question: plant cells tend to have a regular, boxy shape, while animal cells (which have no wall at all) are often round and can bulge into all sorts of shapes. WHY does having a wall force a more regular shape?
Answer: because the wall is RIGID (stiff) — it resists stretching or bulging outward in just one spot. A cell with no wall (like an animal cell) has nothing stopping it from bulging unevenly, so it settles into a rounder, more flexible shape instead.
Key idea: this isn't about spotting boxy-vs-round shapes to identify a cell (you already know that) — it's about explaining WHY the wall causes it, which is the deeper reasoning behind a fact you've already met.
