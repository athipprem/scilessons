Quick Recall from Lesson 1 🔁

🔁 The fire triangle: FUEL + OXYGEN + HEAT, all three at once. Remove any ONE side and the fire goes out. 🔁 Air is mostly nitrogen, but it's the smaller share of OXYGEN that actually supports burning.

Every real fire-fighting method you'll meet today is just "which side of the triangle am I removing?" in disguise.

Why learn this? 🤔

Knowing the fire triangle is only half the job — knowing which method to use, and which methods are dangerous for which type of fire, is what actually keeps people safe. Firefighters, chefs, and even home smoke-alarm designers all rely on exactly this knowledge.
Real life: kitchens keep a fire blanket near the stove (for oil fires, never water); electricians know never to use water near live wires; wildfire crews cut "firebreaks" — bare strips of land with no fuel — to stop a fire's path.

Why Water Isn't ALWAYS the Answer ⚡🛢️

🚫 Overgeneralized claim (FALSE): "Water always puts out every kind of fire." This sounds confident and simple — but it's exactly the kind of sweeping claim that real science almost never supports.

Electrical fires: water conducts electricity. Pouring water on a fire near live wires or plugged-in equipment risks sending a dangerous shock straight back up the water stream to whoever's holding it.

Cooking-oil fires: oil is less dense than water, so water sinks underneath burning oil and instantly turns to steam — violently splashing burning oil outward in every direction, actually spreading the fire instead of stopping it.

Correct, properly-scoped version: "Water puts out most ordinary fires (wood, paper, cloth) by cooling them — but NOT electrical fires or burning oil, which need a different method (cutting power, or smothering with a lid or fire blanket)." Notice how much more trustworthy this sounds than the "always" version — because it actually matches the real exceptions.

Trap-Recognition Drill: Spotting Overgeneralized Claims 🔍

Whenever an answer choice uses a word like ALWAYS, NEVER, ONLY, EVERY, ALL, or COMPLETELY — stop and ask: "is there a real exception to this?" If yes, that option is almost certainly WRONG.

This isn't just a fire-safety trick — it applies to EVERY science topic. Real science is full of exceptions, so a confidently-worded absolute claim is a red flag, not a reassurance. Compare these pairs:

  • 🚫 "Wood is ALWAYS a perfect insulator" vs ✔ "Dry wood is usually a good electrical insulator, though wet or charred wood can conduct"
  • 🚫 "Metal is the BEST material for EVERY situation" vs ✔ "Metal is a good conductor, but that also makes it dangerous to touch near electricity or fire"
  • 🚫 "Water ALWAYS puts out fires" vs ✔ "Water puts out most ordinary fires but not electrical or oil fires"

Everyday Fire Safety Habits 🚪

Smoke alarms: detect smoke early, giving time to react before a fire grows.
Stop, Drop, and Roll: if your clothing catches fire, stop moving (running feeds it more oxygen), drop to the ground, and roll to smother the flames.
Know your exits: always know at least two ways out of a room or building, in case one is blocked by fire or smoke.
Check a door before opening it: if a closed door feels hot, there may be fire on the other side — don't open it; use another exit instead.
Call for help immediately: get everyone out first, then call emergency services from a safe distance — never go back inside for belongings.
