Quick Recall from Units 11 & 22 🔁

🔁 Remember Unit 11? Your body is made of systems that work as organ teams — the digestive system is one of those teams. 🔁 Remember Unit 22? Food contains nutrients (carbs, protein, fats, vitamins, minerals) that your body needs — but getting those nutrients OUT of food and INTO your blood is a whole job of its own.

Today you'll learn exactly HOW your body gets those nutrients out of food — the digestive system's organ-by-organ journey.

Why learn this? 🤔

Every meal you eat has to go on this exact journey before it can give you energy to run, think, or grow. Understanding digestion also explains everyday things — why you feel full, why spicy food can upset your stomach, and why doctors ask about "what you ate" when you're unwell. The Digestive Journey 🚏

Food travels through FIVE main organs, in this exact order, each doing a different job.
The journey: MOUTH (chewing + saliva) → ESOPHAGUS (a muscular tube that squeezes food down) → STOMACH (churns food with strong acid) → SMALL INTESTINE (the long, coiled tube where nutrients pass into your blood) → LARGE INTESTINE (frames around the small intestine, absorbs leftover water).
Organ by organ:
🦷 Mouth: teeth chew food into smaller pieces (mechanical), while saliva starts breaking down starch (chemical) — the very first stage.
🌊 Esophagus: a muscular tube that squeezes food down toward the stomach, like squeezing toothpaste along a tube.
🥣 Stomach: churns food and mixes it with strong digestive juices, turning it into a soupy mixture.
🩸 Small intestine: the walls are covered in millions of tiny finger-like bumps that soak up nutrients into your blood.
💧 Large intestine: absorbs leftover water from what's left, forming solid waste to leave the body.

Why Break Food Down At All? 🔬

Food arrives in big chunks — but only TINY pieces can pass through the intestine wall into your blood.
Two kinds of breaking-down:
🦷 Mechanical — physically breaking food into smaller bits (chewing with teeth, churning in the stomach). This doesn't change WHAT the food is, just its SIZE.
🧪 Chemical — special helper substances called enzymes (found in saliva, stomach juices, and the small intestine) break big food molecules apart into much tinier ones your blood can actually absorb.
Real example: chew a plain cracker for a full minute without swallowing — it starts tasting slightly sweet! That's an enzyme in your saliva already breaking down the cracker's starch into sugar, right there in your mouth.
Remember Unit 15's root hairs? Root hairs give a plant more SURFACE AREA to absorb more water. Your small intestine uses the exact same trick — millions of tiny finger-like bumps massively increase its surface area, so it can absorb nutrients into your blood much faster than a smooth tube ever could.
