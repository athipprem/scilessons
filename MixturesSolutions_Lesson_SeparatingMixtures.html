Fast Recap: Mixture vs Solution 🔁

From Lesson 1: a MIXTURE is two or more substances physically combined. A SOLUTION is a special mixture where a SOLUTE dissolves completely into a SOLVENT. SOLUBLE things dissolve; INSOLUBLE things never do — they just sit mixed in.
Why this matters today: whether a mixture contains a true solution or just insoluble solid pieces completely changes which separating method will actually work.

Why learn this? 🤔

Separating mixtures is one of the most useful skills in all of science — it's how we get clean drinking water, mine metals out of rock, make coffee, and recycle materials instead of throwing everything away together.
Real life: a desalination plant separates drinkable fresh water from salty seawater. A recycling centre separates different plastics by size and density. Even making a cup of tea uses a filter (the tea bag) to keep the leaves out of your cup!

Sieving: Sorting by Size 🧵

SIEVING separates solids of DIFFERENT PARTICLE SIZES using a mesh — small pieces fall through the holes, big pieces stay on top. No liquid needed at all.
A garden sieve separates big stones from fine sand this way — the mesh holes are sized so only the small particles pass through.

Other examples: sifting flour to remove lumps, a colander separating cooked pasta from water (its holes are too small for pasta but let water pass), sorting gravel by size at a quarry.

Filtering: Solid Out of Liquid 🫗

FILTERING separates an INSOLUBLE solid from a liquid, using filter paper with holes far too small for the solid pieces, but big enough to let the liquid through.
Filter paper traps the insoluble sand on top, while clear water — now called the FILTRATE — drips through into the beaker below.
⚠️ Important limit: filtering only catches solid pieces that are still there to be caught. A truly DISSOLVED solute has spread into particles far too small for filter paper to trap — it passes straight through with the liquid, unchanged.

Evaporation: Getting the Solute Back ☀️

EVAPORATION separates a DISSOLVED solid (the solute) from its liquid (the solvent) by heating gently — the solvent turns to gas and escapes into the air, leaving the solute behind as a solid.
Remember from Unit 19: in the water cycle, heat turns liquid water into invisible water vapour — that's the same evaporation, just happening in nature instead of on a stove.
Salt water gently heated in a shallow pan — this is exactly how salt farms near the Thai coast make salt, letting the sun do the evaporating for free.

Key idea: the solute (salt, sugar, etc.) doesn't turn to gas — only the solvent does. That's WHY the solid gets left behind, dry and ready to collect.

Choosing the Right Method 🌳

Has the solid actually DISSOLVED into the liquid (can't see separate pieces, won't settle back out)?
↓
YES — it's a true solution
Use EVAPORATION — heat gently so the solvent turns to vapour and escapes, leaving the dissolved solute behind
NO — it's just mixed in, not dissolved
If it's solid pieces IN A LIQUID, use FILTERING (paper traps the solid, lets liquid through). If it's dry solids of different SIZES with no liquid, use SIEVING (a mesh sorts by size)

Trap-Recognition Drill #4 🚩

Watch for ALWAYS / NEVER / ONLY / ALL in claims about separating mixtures.
Try this one: "Filtering can ALWAYS get a dissolved solid back out of its solution." Is this claim safe to trust?
No. A dissolved solute has spread into particles far too small for filter paper to trap — it slips straight through with the liquid. Filtering only works on solids that HAVEN'T dissolved. To recover a true solute, you need evaporation instead.

The trap isn't that filtering is a bad method — it genuinely works well for insoluble solids. The trap is claiming it works on EVERYTHING, with no exceptions, when dissolved solutions are a real exception.

Worked Example: Ravi's Muddy Jar 🧩

Scenario: Ravi has a jar containing sand, salt, and water — all stirred together at once. He wants clean, dry salt at the end. Design his plan, one step at a time.

Step 1 — check what's in the jar: sand is insoluble (never dissolves); salt is soluble (dissolves completely into the water, forming a true solution with the water).

Step 2 — filter first: pour the mixture through filter paper. The insoluble sand gets trapped on the paper. The filtrate that drips through is salty water — now a clear solution, with the sand fully removed.

Step 3 — evaporate second: gently heat the filtered salty water. The water (solvent) turns to vapour and escapes, leaving dry, clean salt crystals (the solute) behind in the dish.

Why this ORDER matters: if Ravi evaporated first without filtering, the sand would get left behind mixed in with the salt, ruining the clean result — filtering has to come first to remove the insoluble solid, before evaporation deals with the dissolved one.
