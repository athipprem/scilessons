Quick Recall from Lesson 1 & Unit 6 🔁

🔁 Remember Lesson 1? Heat is the total thermal energy in something; temperature is one single number. Water freezes at 0°C, your body is about 37°C, water boils at 100°C. 🔁 Remember Unit 6? Metals are good conductors — of BOTH electricity AND heat. Wood, plastic, and rubber are poor conductors of both (good insulators).

Today: exactly HOW heat travels from one place to another, which materials let it through easily and which block it, and what actually happens to a material at those key temperatures.

Why learn this? 🤔

Choosing the right material for a pot handle, an oven mitt, a winter coat, or a cooler box all comes down to understanding exactly how heat travels and which materials can block it. This shows up constantly in cooking, staying warm or cool, and even in how buildings and spacecraft are designed.
Real life: firefighters wear special heat-resistant suits; a thermos flask is specially built with a gap that blocks all three ways heat can travel, keeping a drink hot OR cold for hours; builders choose insulating materials for walls and roofs to keep houses comfortable without wasting energy.

Three Ways Heat Travels 🔥

Heat always travels from HOTTER things to COOLER things — but it can do this in three different ways: conduction, convection, and radiation.
Conduction: heat passes directly through a touching solid, particle to particle (a metal spoon left in hot soup gets hot at the handle too). Convection: heat moves through a LIQUID or GAS as warmer, less dense parts rise and cooler, denser parts sink, creating a moving current (recall Unit 24's pot of water — this is that same idea, given its proper name). Radiation: heat travels as invisible rays, and — unlike the other two — it doesn't need to touch anything or have any material in between at all.
The giveaway for radiation: it's the ONLY one of the three that can cross completely empty space (a vacuum) — that's exactly how the Sun's heat reaches Earth across millions of kilometres of empty space, and how you feel a campfire's warmth on your face without touching it.

Blocking Heat, and What Heat Does to Materials 🧤

Good heat CONDUCTORS let heat pass through quickly (metals). Good heat INSULATORS slow heat down (wood, plastic, rubber, and even trapped air).
Why this matters for everyday objects: saucepan handles are wood or plastic, not metal, so YOU don't get burned by conducted heat. Oven mitts are thick with lots of trapped air inside — air itself is a poor conductor, so a fluffy pocket of trapped air blocks heat really well. A thermos flask uses an insulating gap (often with trapped air or even a vacuum) to slow down all three ways heat can travel at once.
Heating and cooling a material can also change its STATE — solid, liquid, or gas — and this always happens at a specific, fixed temperature for that substance.

Melting point = the temperature where a SOLID turns into a LIQUID (ice melting into water).
Freezing point = the temperature where a LIQUID turns into a SOLID (water freezing into ice).
Boiling point = the temperature where a LIQUID turns into a GAS (water boiling into steam).

Sneaky but important: for any one substance, the melting point and freezing point are the EXACT SAME temperature — just going in opposite directions. Water melts AND freezes at exactly 0°C; whether it's called "melting" or "freezing" just depends on which direction the change is happening (solid→liquid, or liquid→solid). Water boils at 100°C, from Lesson 1's thermometer screen.

How to use this: first ask if anything is TOUCHING (conduction). If not, ask if it's moving through a liquid or gas as a current (convection). If it can cross empty space with nothing touching or flowing at all, it's radiation.

1. Which of the three ways heat travels is the ONLY one that can cross completely empty space?

✔ Correct — radiation is the only one that doesn't need any material to travel through.
✘ Re-check Screen 4 — only radiation can cross a complete vacuum, like the space between the Sun and Earth.

2. A metal ladle is left resting inside a pot of hot stew. After a few minutes, the handle (which never touched the stew directly) feels hot too. What is this an example of?

✔ Correct — heat conducted along the touching metal, from the hot part to the handle.
✘ Re-check Screen 4 — heat passing through a solid, touching object is conduction.

3. A bowl of soup is heated on a stove. Warmer soup rises to the top while cooler soup sinks to the bottom, over and over, until it's evenly hot. What is this called?

✔ Correct — this rising-and-sinking current in a liquid or gas is convection.
✘ Re-check Screen 4 — warm rising, cool sinking, forming a current in a liquid or gas, is convection.

4. Why are oven mitts usually thick and padded, rather than thin like a single layer of fabric?

✔ Correct — more trapped air means better insulation, since air is a poor conductor of heat.
✘ Re-check Screen 5 — the thickness matters because it traps more air, and trapped air blocks heat well.

5. A block of ice is left out and melts into water at 0°C. Later, that same water is put in a freezer and turns back into ice. At what temperature does it freeze?

✔ Correct — melting point and freezing point are the same temperature for a given substance, just in opposite directions.
✘ Re-check Screen 5 — for any substance, the melting point and freezing point are the exact same temperature.
