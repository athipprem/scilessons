Quick Fix from Unit 41 🧪

Unit 41's test found a mix-up between everyday ACIDS and BASES — three separate questions all pointed the same way. Two fresh, ungraded checks before we move on to circuits.

Warm-up. A cleaning spray label says: "feels a bit slippery on your hands, and turns red litmus paper blue." Is this spray an acid or a base?

✔ Correct — slippery feel and turning red litmus blue are both classic BASE signs. Soap and baking soda are everyday bases, even though neither one tastes sour.
✘ Re-think it: an ACID turns blue litmus RED. This spray turned RED litmus BLUE — that's the opposite direction, the base signature.

Warm-up. A student mixes vinegar with baking soda and it fizzes. Vinegar is sour-tasting and turns blue litmus red. Baking soda feels slightly slippery and turns red litmus blue. What TWO types of substance just combined?

✔ Correct — vinegar (sour, turns blue litmus red) is an ACID. Baking soda (slippery, turns red litmus blue) is a BASE. The fizzing happens when an acid meets a base.
✘ Re-think it: look at what each substance does to litmus paper. One turns blue→red (that's the acid signature); the other turns red→blue (that's the base signature) — they're not the same type.

Quick Warm-Up 🔁

This is an UNGRADED refresher pulling from Units 27-28 — Earth's tilt and the seasons — content you haven't seen tested in a while. Just to keep it fresh.

Warm-up. Does Earth's axis change how much it's tilted at different times of the year?

✔ Correct — Earth's axis stays tilted about 23.5° all year round, always pointing the same direction in space. What changes is WHERE Earth is in its orbit, which decides which hemisphere leans toward the Sun.
✘ Re-think it: nothing about the tilt ITSELF changes — Earth just moves around the Sun while the tilt stays fixed, always pointing the same way.

Warm-up. Is Earth's changing distance from the Sun the real reason we have seasons?

✔ Correct — it's the TILT that matters, not distance. A tilted hemisphere facing the Sun gets more direct sunlight and longer days, which is what actually causes summer.
✘ Re-think it: Unit 28's big idea was that distance is a myth — it's the tilt of Earth's axis that causes seasons, not how close Earth happens to be to the Sun.

Why learn this? 🤔

You already know a circuit needs a complete loop to work. But real gadgets often have MORE than one bulb or component — and HOW they're connected changes everything about how the whole thing behaves.
📋 ASMOPSS connection: circuit diagrams are a recurring favourite on real papers — you'll often be shown a diagram and asked to predict what happens if one part fails, which is exactly what Units 42's two lessons build toward.
Real life: old-style Christmas lights sometimes had a famous, annoying problem — if ONE bulb burned out, the ENTIRE string went dark, and you had to test every bulb one by one to find the broken one. By the end of Lesson 2, you'll know exactly why that happened.

Quick Recap: Complete Circuits 🔁

From Unit 6: a circuit needs a POWER SOURCE, a PATH (wires), and something to USE the energy — all connected in ONE unbroken loop. A gap ANYWHERE breaks the whole loop.
From Units 6 & 34: a CONDUCTOR lets electricity flow through it easily (like copper wire); an INSULATOR blocks it (like plastic or rubber). A broken wire, a flat battery, or an open switch all create the same kind of gap.
Today we go further: what happens when there's MORE THAN ONE bulb in the circuit, and they're all connected one after another in a single chain?

What Is a Series Circuit? 🔗

A SERIES circuit connects everything — battery and every bulb — along ONE single loop, one after another, with no branches.
Follow the wire with your finger: battery → bulb 1 → bulb 2 → bulb 3 → back to battery. There's only ONE path — every bulb sits along that same single chain, one after another.
Key idea: in a series circuit, electricity has no choice but to pass through EVERY bulb, one at a time, because there's only one road and no branches off it.

Series Circuits: What Happens When One Bulb Fails? 💥

If ANY bulb in a series circuit breaks (or is removed), the WHOLE loop is broken — EVERY bulb in the circuit goes out, not just the broken one.

Remember Unit 6's rule: a gap ANYWHERE breaks the whole loop. A series circuit is just one long loop with several bulbs sitting on it — so a broken bulb IS a gap in that one and only path. With the path broken, nothing can complete the loop, so every bulb on it loses power at the same time.

Bonus fact — brightness: the more bulbs you add to a series circuit, the DIMMER each one glows, because they're all sharing the same single stream of electricity between them. Three bulbs in series are each dimmer than just one bulb alone on the same battery.

Worked Example: The Old Christmas Lights Problem 🎄

Scenario: An old string of 20 Christmas lights is wired as ONE series circuit — every bulb sits along a single loop, one after another. One bulb's filament breaks.

Step 1 — is this circuit still complete? No. The broken filament is a gap, and it sits directly on the ONLY path the electricity has.

Step 2 — what happens to the other 19 bulbs? They ALL go dark too, even though they're perfectly fine — because the single shared loop is broken, none of them can complete their circuit either.

Step 3 — why is this so annoying to fix? You can't just look at the string and see which bulb is broken — you have to test bulbs one at a time until you find the one gap breaking the whole loop.

Conclusion: this is the real-world cost of a series circuit — simple to build, but ONE failure anywhere takes down everything connected to that same loop.
