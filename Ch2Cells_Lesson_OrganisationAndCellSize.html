Quick Warm-Up 🔁

This is an UNGRADED refresher pulling from Unit 13 — light and shadows — content you haven't seen tested since 10 Jul 2026. Just to keep it fresh, and it's the exact idea behind today's Lesson 2 (the microscope's light).

Warm-up. A single small torch bulb, held far from a wall, shines on a pencil standing upright on a table. What kind of shadow edge will the pencil cast?

✔ Correct — a small, far-away bulb behaves like a POINT source, so the shadow has one sharp, crisp edge with no penumbra.
✘ Re-think it: does a point source (light coming from just one tiny spot) give a sharp shadow or a fuzzy one?

Warm-up. A long fluorescent tube light now shines on the same pencil instead of the torch. Compared to the torch's shadow, will this shadow's edge be sharper or softer?

✔ Correct — a wide, BROAD source (like a fluorescent tube) sends light from many spots at once, so the shadow gets a softer, fuzzier penumbra fringe.
✘ Re-think it: a broad source sends light rays from many different spots across its width — does that sharpen or soften a shadow's edge?

Quick Fix from Unit 46 🌱

On your real Unit 46 test, three misses (Q4, Q9, Q19) shared one gap: telling seeds (sexual — needs pollination, genetically DIFFERENT offspring) apart from spores and other plant parts (both asexual, genetically IDENTICAL offspring). Two quick fresh checks before we move on.

Quick Fix (mechanism). An onion bulb splits in half underground, and each half grows into a full new onion plant. Meanwhile, a bee carries pollen from one cherry tree's flower to another, and the pollinated flower grows into a cherry containing a seed. Which of these two new plants will be genetically IDENTICAL to its parent?

✔ Correct — splitting a bulb is ASEXUAL (no pollination involved), so it copies the parent exactly. The seed came from pollination — a SEXUAL method — so it's genetically DIFFERENT from either parent.
✘ Re-think it: which of the two methods involved pollen moving from one flower to another (sexual), and which one didn't (asexual)?

Quick Fix (spot the flaw). A classmate claims: "A plant grown from a seed, and a plant grown by splitting a potato tuber, will ALWAYS be genetically identical to their parent — because both are just plants growing from a plant part." Is this claim correct?

✔ Correct — this claim wrongly lumps SEXUAL (seed) and ASEXUAL (tuber-splitting) reproduction together just because both start from "a plant part." Only the asexual method gives an identical copy. You'll find out exactly WHY asexual copies are identical — right down to the level of chromosomes — in a future unit.
✘ Re-think it: does a SEED always come from pollination (sexual), even when it's technically "a plant part"?

Why learn this? 🤔

Your school class is starting Chapter 2, Section A of your i-Science book: Cells as building blocks. This unit matches it — but goes past it, into two ideas your book never spells out.
Real life: it's why a whale isn't just a scaled-up mouse with giant cells — it's built from far more of the SAME-sized cells. It's why a cut on your finger heals starting from the edges inward, tissue by tissue. And it's exactly why a doctor looking down a microscope can tell a muscle sample from a skin sample at a glance.

The Organisation Ladder 🪜

NEW idea: your book stops at "cells are the building blocks." This is the STRUCTURE those blocks build — five rungs, smallest to biggest: CELL → TISSUE → ORGAN → ORGAN SYSTEM → ORGANISM.
Each rung is a team built from the rung below it: cells team up into a tissue, tissues team up into an organ, organs team up into an organ system, and organ systems team up into the whole organism.
Worked example — you: a muscle CELL → many identical muscle cells make muscle TISSUE → muscle tissue plus other tissues make the heart, an ORGAN → the heart plus blood vessels make the circulatory ORGAN SYSTEM → all your organ systems together make YOU, the whole ORGANISM.
Worked example — a tree: a leaf CELL → many leaf cells make leaf TISSUE → leaf tissue plus other tissues make a leaf, an ORGAN → the leaf plus stems and branches make the shoot ORGAN SYSTEM → the whole plant is the ORGANISM.
Key idea — connecting to Unit 11: back in Unit 11 you learned about organ systems (like the digestive and circulatory systems), each made of organs working as a team — that's the TOP TWO rungs of this ladder. Unit 11 never named the bottom two rungs at all: the cell, and the tissue (a group of the SAME kind of cell working together). Today fills that gap in.

Why Cells Are Small 📐

NEW idea: a cell takes in food and oxygen through its SURFACE, but has to supply its whole VOLUME. As a cell grows bigger, its surface can't keep up with its volume — so cells stay small, and big organisms are built from MORE cells, not BIGGER ones.
Try it yourself. Pick a cube size below and watch what happens to its surface area, volume, and the ratio between them.
Pick a cube size above to see its numbers.
Key idea: doubling a cube's side makes its SURFACE go up 4×, but its VOLUME go up 8× — the surface falls further and further behind. A giant single cell couldn't get enough food and oxygen in through its tiny (relatively speaking) surface to feed its huge volume. So instead of growing one giant cell, living things grow MORE cells, each one staying small enough to feed itself.
Connecting to Unit 44: this is exactly WHY a ten-year-old is taller mainly because of MORE cells from division (Unit 44), not because each cell has individually swelled up huge — an oversized cell would starve itself. Growing by dividing into more small cells, instead of growing one huge cell, is the only way that actually works.

One Cell, Every Job 🔬

Higher-tier question: a UNICELLULAR organism — like an amoeba — has no rungs above "cell." No tissue, no organ, no organ system. So which jobs still need doing, and who does them?
Answer: ALL of them. A unicellular organism's one single cell must do EVERY job itself — moving, feeding, sensing its surroundings, and getting rid of waste — jobs that in you are split across many different specialised tissues, organs, and organ systems.
Key idea: an amoeba has exactly ONE rung of the ladder — "cell" — and that same single cell also counts as the whole "organism." It skips tissue, organ, and organ system entirely; there's simply nothing above the cell rung to skip TO.
