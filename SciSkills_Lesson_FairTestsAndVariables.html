Quick Recall from Units 4, 20, 30 & 35 🔁

🔁 Remember Unit 4 (Mould & Fair Tests)? A fair test changes ONLY one thing on purpose and keeps everything else the same. 🔁 Remember Unit 35? The independent variable is the ONE thing changed on purpose. The dependent variable is what you measure as the result. The controlled variables are everything else, kept the same on purpose so the test stays fair.

Those rules work perfectly when there are just TWO groups. But ASMOPSS loves setups with THREE, FOUR, or even FIVE groups at once — and in those, it's easy to lose track of which group is testing what. That's exactly what today fixes.

Why learn this? 🤔

Real scientists almost never test just two groups — a new medicine might be tested against three different doses PLUS a no-medicine group, all at once. Doctors, farmers, and engineers all have to read multi-group experiments and correctly say which group proves which fact. Getting this wrong in real life can mean believing something works when it doesn't (or the reverse)!
Real life: a farmer testing 4 different fertilisers on 4 identical rows of crops; a doctor's drug trial with a no-treatment group, a low-dose group, and a high-dose group; a phone company testing 3 battery designs under the exact same conditions to see which lasts longest.

Why Multi-Group Setups Trip People Up 🧩

With only 2 groups, there's just ONE difference to spot. With 3+ groups, EACH group might change something DIFFERENT — so you can no longer compare "everything" to "everything," you must compare each group to the control, ONE AT A TIME.

Example set-up: Three identical leaves — X, Y, and Z — are all tested for starch after a day outside.

  • Leaf X: left completely untouched, normal light, normal air, all day.
  • Leaf Y: had black paper covering HALF of it all day (the other half stayed uncovered).
  • Leaf Z: had its carbon dioxide supply completely removed with a special chemical, but was left in full light.

If you try to compare all three at once in your head, it's easy to get confused about which leaf is proving what. The fix: treat it like a table, one row per group.

The Clue Table Method 📋

STEP 1: Find the group that is completely normal, untouched — that's your CONTROL. STEP 2: For every OTHER group, ask "what is the ONE thing different from the control?" STEP 3: That ONE difference tells you exactly what that group is testing.
GroupWhat's different from the control?What this group tests
Leaf XNothing — normal light, normal airThis is the CONTROL — the "everything normal" comparison point
Leaf YHalf was blocked from LIGHTWhether LIGHT is needed for that half to make starch
Leaf ZCARBON DIOXIDE was removed (light stayed normal)Whether CARBON DIOXIDE is needed to make starch
Key rule: once you've built the table, answer the question ONE ROW AT A TIME. Never try to compare all the groups to each other directly — always compare each group back to the control, one at a time. That's the whole trick.
What you'd expect to find: Leaf X (the control) tests blue-black everywhere — it had everything it needed. Leaf Y tests blue-black only on the half that got light — the covered half stayed brown (no starch, because no light reached it). Leaf Z tests brown everywhere — even though it had light, with no carbon dioxide it couldn't make any starch at all.

Spotting the Control in ANY Multi-Group Set-Up 🌳

Look at each group. Has ANYTHING about it been changed from what's normal?
No — this group is completely normal/untouched
THIS IS THE CONTROL
Yes — exactly ONE thing about it is different
THIS GROUP TESTS THAT ONE THING
Warning sign: if you ever find a group where TWO or more things changed at once compared to the control (like temperature AND amount of water both changing together), that group is NOT a fair test on its own — flag it, because you can't tell which change caused the result.

Second Worked Example: Seed Germination 🌱

Set-up: Four identical trays of bean seeds are prepared. Tray A: normal soil, normal water, normal room temperature (20°C), left in a sunlit spot. Tray B: same as Tray A, but placed in a dark cupboard instead. Tray C: same as Tray A, but given NO water at all. Tray D: same as Tray A, but kept in a cold fridge (4°C) instead of room temperature.

GroupWhat's different from Tray A?What this group tests
Tray ANothing — this is normalCONTROL
Tray BNo light (dark cupboard)Whether LIGHT is needed for germination
Tray CNo water at allWhether WATER is needed for germination
Tray DCold temperature (4°C)Whether WARMTH is needed for germination
The payoff: seeds actually germinate WITHOUT light (Tray B would still sprout) but NOT without water or warmth (Trays C and D would fail). Once you've isolated exactly what each tray tests, you can predict — and explain — the results with confidence, instead of guessing.
