Quick Recap 🔁

From Lesson 1: a SERIES circuit connects everything along ONE single shared loop. If any bulb fails, the whole loop breaks and EVERY bulb goes out. More bulbs in series also means each one glows dimmer, since they share the same stream of electricity.
Today: what if each bulb had its OWN separate path back to the battery instead of sharing just one? That's a completely different kind of circuit.

What Is a Parallel Circuit? 🔀

A PARALLEL circuit gives each bulb its OWN separate branch, all connecting back to the same battery — like several small loops sharing one power source.
Follow just Bulb 2's branch with your finger: battery → bulb 2 → back to battery. That branch works completely on its own — it doesn't need bulb 1 or bulb 3 to be working at all.
Key idea: in a parallel circuit, each bulb sits on its own mini-loop. All the branches share the same battery, but each branch is its own separate, complete path.

Parallel Circuits: What Happens When One Bulb Fails? 💡

If ONE bulb in a parallel circuit breaks, ONLY that bulb's own branch is affected — every OTHER bulb keeps working normally, because each branch is its own complete loop.

A broken bulb creates a gap — but only on its OWN branch. The other branches never touched that gap, so their loops are still complete, and electricity keeps flowing through them exactly as before.

Bonus fact — brightness: because each bulb has its own separate path straight back to the battery (not shared with the others), each bulb in a parallel circuit shines at its normal, full brightness — adding more branches doesn't dim the existing ones the way adding more bulbs in series does.

Trap-Recognition Drill #6 🚩

A tempting shortcut hides in what you just learned. Let's catch it before it catches you.

Warm-up. A circuit has more than one bulb connected to a battery. Does having MORE THAN ONE bulb always mean the circuit must be parallel?

✔ Correct — the NUMBER of bulbs doesn't decide series vs parallel. What decides it is whether they share ONE loop (series) or each have their OWN separate branch (parallel). Lesson 1's series circuit had three bulbs!
✘ Re-think it: Lesson 1's series circuit diagram had THREE bulbs, all sharing one loop. Having multiple bulbs doesn't automatically make a circuit parallel.
Key idea: "more than one bulb = parallel" is a trap. Ask instead: does each bulb have its own separate branch, or do they all share one single path? That's the real test.

Worked Example: Old Christmas Lights vs a House 🎄🏠

Scenario A — old-style Christmas lights: wired as ONE series circuit, all 20 bulbs on a single shared loop.
Step 1: one bulb's filament breaks — a gap on the ONLY path.
Step 2: all 20 bulbs go dark, because the one shared loop is broken.

Scenario B — a real house: every room's lights are wired in PARALLEL, each on its own separate branch back to the main supply.
Step 1: the kitchen light bulb burns out — a gap, but only on the kitchen's own branch.
Step 2: every other room's lights stay on completely normally, because their branches were never affected.

Conclusion: real houses use parallel wiring on purpose — nobody wants the whole house to go dark just because one bulb burned out in one room!
