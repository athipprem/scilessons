Today we go one level deeper: what's the actual DIFFERENCE between "heat" and "temperature" — two words people (and even some test questions!) mix up — and how do scientists measure temperature properly?

Why learn this? 🤔

Heat and temperature show up everywhere: checking if you have a fever, following a recipe's oven temperature, dressing for the weather, or an engineer choosing materials that can survive extreme heat or cold. ASMOPSS papers ask heat/temperature questions regularly — it's one of the most frequently recurring topics across past papers.
Real life: a nurse uses a thermometer to check your temperature when you're sick; a chef relies on the oven's temperature dial to cook food safely; engineers test how bridge materials expand and contract across a whole year's range of temperatures before choosing them.

Heat vs Temperature: Not the Same Thing! 🌡️

TEMPERATURE is how hot or cold something is — one single number. HEAT is the TOTAL amount of thermal energy inside something, added up across every single particle.

A useful comparison: imagine a small cup of hot tea (95°C) and a huge bathtub full of warm water (35°C).

  • The tea has a HIGHER temperature — each particle, on average, is moving faster.
  • But the bathtub has MORE heat overall — there are so many more particles in all that water, even though each one is moving a bit slower on average.

If you poured the tea into the bathtub, the huge tub would barely warm up at all — but the tiny cup of tea would cool down a LOT. That's because the bathtub's much bigger total heat energy overwhelms the tea's smaller total, even though the tea started off hotter.

Key rule: TEMPERATURE = how fast particles are moving, on average (one number). HEAT = the total thermal energy of ALL the particles added together (depends on both temperature AND how much stuff there is).

Measuring Temperature: The Celsius Scale 📏

Scientists measure temperature with a THERMOMETER, using a scale called degrees Celsius (°C).
Three reference points worth knowing by heart: water freezes into ice at 0°C, a healthy human body is about 37°C, and water boils into steam at 100°C. A warm day in Thailand is usually around 30-35°C.
No conversions needed: we always use Celsius (°C) — not Fahrenheit. Converting between the two involves a formula that's beyond what you need for ASMOPSS; just get comfortable reading and comparing Celsius numbers directly.

How to use this: if a question just asks "how hot is it?" or gives you a thermometer reading, that's temperature. If a question talks about total energy, or compares a big amount of something to a small amount at a similar temperature, that's heat.
