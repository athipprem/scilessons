1. You're roasting sausages over a portable gas burner at a campsite. Hot fat drips into the pan and catches fire. What's your safest immediate action?

✔ Correct — cutting the fuel supply (gas off) and covering the pan (removes oxygen) is safe. Water on a fat fire makes it splash and spread.
✘ Not quite — water splashes burning fat and spreads the fire. Turning off the heat and covering the pan (cutting off oxygen) is the safe move, in a kitchen, a campsite, or anywhere else.

Quick Recall from Unit 25 & Unit 32 🔁

🔁 Remember the flower teams? Every flower part belongs to one of two teams: the STAMEN (male — anther makes pollen, filament holds it up) or the PISTIL (female — stigma catches pollen, style is the tube, ovary holds the ovule(s)). 🔁 Remember the pathway? Pollen leaves the ANTHER → lands on the STIGMA (pollination) → travels down the style and joins an OVULE inside the OVARY (fertilization).

You've nailed that pathway solidly across two units now. Today picks up exactly where it left off: fertilization just happened — so what happens NEXT?

Why learn this? 🤔

Every fruit and vegetable stall, every recipe, and even a recent real competition question all hinge on this one idea. Get it solid, and a whole category of "is this a fruit or a vegetable" trick questions stops being tricky at all.
Real life: a grocer sorting tomatoes into the "vegetable" section by habit, even though a botanist would put them with the fruit; a farmer knowing that removing flowers before they're pollinated means no fruit will ever form there.

Ovary → Fruit, Ovule → Seed 🌸➡️🍎

After fertilization: the OVARY WALL swells and develops into the FRUIT. Each OVULE inside develops into a SEED.
The ovary wall (green, left) swells into the fruit (red, right) — and every ovule inside it (small green dots) becomes a seed (dark dots). Same structures, new jobs.
Key rule: the OVULE already contains the fertilized egg that will grow into a brand-new plant — so it becomes the SEED. The OVARY WALL around it just forms a case that protects the seeds and, in many plants, later helps them travel — so it becomes the FRUIT.

Try it: a carrot — did it grow from a fertilized ovary? No, it's a swollen ROOT. First question answers "No" immediately, so a carrot is NOT a fruit, no matter how sweet it tastes.

The Kitchen Trap: Fruit vs "Vegetable" 🍅

Watch out: "sweet" and "fruit" are NOT the same thing in science. The ONLY rule is: did it grow from a fertilized ovary, and does it hold seeds?

Scientifically true fruits (even though people call them "vegetables" in the kitchen): tomato, cucumber, pumpkin, pea pod, bell pepper, avocado — every single one grew from a flower's ovary and holds seeds inside.

True vegetables (never fruits, because they never came from an ovary): carrot (a swollen ROOT), potato (a swollen underground STEM), celery (a leaf STALK), spinach and lettuce (LEAVES).

Watch out: "it tastes sweet, so it must be a fruit" and "it's savory, so it can't be a fruit" are both traps. Taste has nothing to do with the rule — only the ovary-and-seeds check does.

Worked Example: Slicing Open an Apple 🍎

Set-up: You slice an apple in half and see a star-shaped pattern of small seeds in the middle, surrounded by the juicy white flesh, surrounded by the red skin.

Wrong explanation (a trap!): "The seeds and the flesh are the same part, they just look different in places." This ignores that they came from two completely different original structures.
Correct explanation: the small seeds in the middle were the OVULES before fertilization — each one grew into a seed. The juicy flesh and skin around them were the OVARY WALL — it swelled up into the fruit that protects and surrounds those seeds. Two different starting parts, doing two different jobs, now sitting inside the same apple.
🧠 Habit to build: whenever you look at a fruit, mentally split it into "the seeds" (were ovules) and "everything else around them" (was the ovary wall) — that split works on almost any fruit you'll ever see.
