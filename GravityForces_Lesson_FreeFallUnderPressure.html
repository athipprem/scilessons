Fast Recap: Gravity & Air Resistance 🔁

Without air resistance, EVERY object falls at exactly the same rate, no matter its mass. WITH air resistance, an object's SHAPE and SURFACE AREA can slow its fall — a flat, spread-out shape catches more air than a compact, heavy one.
Remember from Unit 21: a bowling ball and a feather dropped together in true vacuum land at the same instant. Drop them in normal air instead, and the feather's large, spread-out shape catches far more air resistance relative to its tiny weight, so it drifts down slowly while the bowling ball falls almost as if the air wasn't there at all.

Two flat sheets of paper — one crumpled into a tight ball, one left flat — have the exact same MASS. Which one lands first, and why?

Answer: the crumpled ball lands first. Crumpling doesn't change its mass at all — it only shrinks its surface area, so it catches far less air resistance than the flat sheet on the way down.

The Stress Test: Three at Once 🎯

Real exam questions rarely compare just two objects — sometimes you have to rank THREE or more, all at once, without losing track.
A golf ball, a flat paper plate, and that SAME plate crumpled into a tight ball are all released together from the same height. Two of the three (golf ball, crumpled plate) are both small and compact; only the flat plate spreads out.
Work it through ONE object at a time, don't rush: the golf ball is small and dense (little air resistance). The crumpled plate is also small and compact now (little air resistance). The FLAT plate is spread wide open — lots of air resistance. So the golf ball and crumpled plate land at nearly the same time, first; the flat plate lands last, clearly behind them.

Trap-Recognition Drill #2 🚩

Watch for ALWAYS / NEVER / ONLY / ALL in claims about gravity and falling objects — the same trap that cost real exam marks on Q6, Q9, Q26, and Q28.
Try this one: "Heavier objects ALWAYS fall faster than lighter ones — no exceptions." Is this claim safe to trust?
No. It's only true when air resistance affects the two objects differently. Drop a bowling ball and a feather TOGETHER in a vacuum, with no air at all, and they fall at exactly the same rate — the claim's "no exceptions" wording makes it false.

The trap isn't the idea of a heavier object seeming to fall faster — that can genuinely happen in real air. The trap is claiming it ALWAYS happens, with no exceptions, when a real exception (true vacuum, or negligible air resistance) exists.

Worked Example: The Coin and the Card 🪙

Scenario: a 1-baht coin and a flat playing card are held at the same height and dropped together. A student says, "The coin is heavier, so it always lands first — end of story." A second student disagrees.

Step 1 — check for air resistance difference: the coin is small, thick, and compact. The card is thin and spread out flat. Their SHAPES are very different, so air resistance affects them very differently.

Step 2 — apply the rule correctly: because air resistance really is different here, the coin lands first — but NOT simply because it's heavier. It lands first because its compact shape catches far less air resistance than the card's flat, spread-out shape.

Step 3 — spot the trap: the first student's reasoning ("heavier always wins, end of story") happened to reach the right answer for the wrong reason. If you turned that same card edge-on and dropped it point-down, it would fall almost as fast as the coin — same weight, same materials, totally different result — because shape and air resistance are the real cause, not weight alone.
