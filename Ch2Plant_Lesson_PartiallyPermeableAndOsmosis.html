Quick Recap 🔁

From Lesson 1: the wall lets EVERYTHING through — it's fully permeable. The MEMBRANE, sitting just inside it, is the real chooser about what enters and leaves the cell.
Today: exactly HOW does the membrane choose? And what happens when the thing moving through is water itself?

What "Partially Permeable" Means 🧊

NEW idea: the membrane has holes so small that WATER can pass through, but bigger dissolved particles (like sugar) can't. That's called being PARTIALLY permeable — only some things get through.
Start with a sieve you already know: a kitchen sieve has holes that let both water AND dissolved sugar pass straight through together — a sieve can't tell the two apart at all.
Now break that picture: a partially permeable membrane is NOT like a kitchen sieve. Its holes are so small that only tiny water particles fit through — a dissolved sugar particle is too big, and stays trapped on its own side.
Key idea: "partially permeable" doesn't mean "half-open" — it means SELECTIVE: small particles like water pass, bigger dissolved particles don't.

Osmosis 💧

NEW idea, never taught before: OSMOSIS is water moving through a partially permeable membrane, from a side with MORE water (fewer dissolved particles) to a side with LESS water (more dissolved particles) — until both sides even out.

Key idea: the dissolved particles themselves CANNOT cross the membrane — only water can. Water always moves toward the side that has proportionally less of it (more crowded with dissolved particles), diluting that side, until both sides have an equal balance.

Turgor & Wilting — the Mechanism 🌱

You already know a plant wilts when it loses water (Unit 14). Here's WHY, mechanically.
Turgid: when a plant cell takes in water by osmosis, its vacuole swells and pushes outward against the rigid wall. A cell in this firm, full state is called TURGID — and a plant made of turgid cells stands up straight.
Flaccid: when a cell loses water instead, the vacuole shrinks and stops pressing against the wall. A cell in this limp state is called FLACCID — and a plant made of flaccid cells wilts.
Unit 14 told you a plant wilts when its cells lose water. Now you know the mechanism underneath that fact: losing water means losing turgor, and a flaccid cell can't hold the plant upright.

Key idea: celery in plain water has MORE water outside its cells than inside, so water moves IN by osmosis and the celery crisps back up. Celery in very salty water has LESS water outside (the salt takes up the "room"), so water moves OUT instead, and the celery goes even limper. Same rule, opposite outcome — this is exactly what happens to a gardener's plant if too much fertiliser or salt gets into the soil.

Two Ways to See Osmosis Direction 🔗

Concrete route — a picture you can imagine

Picture a very CROWDED room next to a completely EMPTY room, joined by a doorway that only PEOPLE can fit through (not the room's furniture). People naturally drift from the crowded room into the empty one, spreading out, until both rooms feel about equally full. Water does the same thing — it drifts from the side with more of it into the side with less, through the membrane's doorway.

Two Ways to See Osmosis Direction — Route 2 🔗

Same idea, now as a step-by-step rule instead of a picture.
Procedural route — the rule, one step at a time
  1. Step 1: Which side has MORE water (fewer dissolved particles)?
  2. Step 2: Which side has LESS water (more dissolved particles)?
  3. Step 3: Water moves from the MORE side to the LESS side.
  4. Step 4: Stop when both sides are equal.
Open question — carried to Unit 50: a plant cell can take in a huge amount of water by osmosis and just get nice and firm. An animal cell (no wall at all) taking in the same amount of water would actually BURST. Why do you think the wall makes that difference? Keep it in mind — Unit 50 answers it properly.

Trap-Recognition Drill #12 🚨

Watch for words like ALWAYS, NEVER, ONLY, and EVERY. A claim using one of these words is often too extreme to be true.

Drill. A student claims: "Osmosis ONLY happens in plants." Is this claim true?

✔ Correct — this is a false absolute claim (the word "ONLY" is the giveaway). Osmosis happens wherever there's a partially permeable membrane with water on both sides — that includes animal cells too, not just plants.
✘ Re-think it: does osmosis need a plant CELL WALL specifically, or just any partially permeable membrane?
