Quick Recap 🔁

From Lesson 1: an ACID turns blue litmus red; a BASE turns red litmus blue; NEUTRAL changes neither.
From Unit 9 (Reactions) and Unit 40 (Separating Materials): a PHYSICAL change doesn't make a new substance — it just changes how something looks, its state, or its arrangement, and it's usually reversible. Dissolving sugar in water is a physical change: the sugar is still sugar, just spread out too small to see.
Today we go deeper: exactly WHAT evidence proves a change is chemical, not physical — and where a full 10-year-old's-eye-view can still be fooled.

Physical Change, Revisited 🧊

A PHYSICAL change alters an object's size, shape, or state — but it's still made of the exact same substance, and it can usually be undone.
Melting ice is still 100% water, just in a different state — and it's easy to reverse by freezing it again. That reversibility is a strong clue it's physical, not chemical.

More physical changes: tearing paper, dissolving sugar, crushing a can, boiling water into steam, cutting an apple into slices.

Chemical Change: The Real Evidence 💥

A CHEMICAL change makes a genuinely NEW substance with different properties — and it's usually hard or impossible to undo.

Four solid clues something might be a chemical change:

🎨
Colour change that isn't just mixing — e.g. an iron nail rusting from grey to orange-brown.
🫧
Bubbles or gas appearing from a reaction (not from boiling! more on that next screen).
🌡️
Temperature change on its own, with no outside heating or cooling — e.g. a chemical hand-warmer getting hot by itself.
👃
A new smell appearing that wasn't there in either starting substance — e.g. the smell of burnt toast.
Key idea: the strongest clue of all is whether it's easy to reverse. Rust can't be un-rusted back into a shiny nail by hand — that permanence is the real signature of a chemical change.

Trap-Recognition Drill #5 🚩

One of the four clues from the last screen has a trap hiding inside it. Let's catch it before it catches you.

Warm-up. A pot of water bubbles vigorously as it boils on the stove. Does bubbling ALWAYS mean a chemical change is happening?

✔ Correct — boiling water bubbles because it's turning to steam, a physical change you can reverse by cooling it back to liquid. Bubbles alone aren't proof of a chemical change; you need to check what KIND of bubbles and whether a new substance actually formed.
✘ Re-think it: what's actually happening inside a pot of plain boiling water — is a new substance forming, or is the water just changing state?
Key idea: "bubbles = chemical change" is a trap. Ask instead: is a genuinely NEW substance forming, and would it be hard to undo? Boiling water fails both tests — it's still just water, and freezing brings it right back.

Chemical or Physical? 🌳

Is a genuinely NEW substance forming (new colour, smell, gas from a reaction, or heat with no outside source) — AND is it hard to undo?
↓
YES to both
CHEMICAL change (e.g. rusting, burning, baking a cake)
NO — same substance, easy to reverse
PHYSICAL change (e.g. melting, dissolving, tearing, boiling water alone)
Key idea: check BOTH conditions, not just one — a state change can look dramatic (like boiling) without being chemical at all.

Worked Example: Vinegar Meets Baking Soda 🌋

Scenario: A student pours vinegar (an ACID) onto a spoonful of baking soda (a BASE) in a cup.

Step 1 — what happens immediately? The mixture fizzes and bubbles vigorously, and the cup feels slightly cooler to the touch.

Step 2 — is the fizzing a trap, like boiling water? No — this time, check the other clues too: a genuinely new gas (carbon dioxide) is being produced by the acid and base reacting together, not just a state change of one single substance.

Step 3 — can you get the original vinegar and baking soda back by just waiting or cooling it? No — once they've reacted, you can't un-react them back into plain vinegar and baking soda. That's the chemical-change signature.

Step 4 — conclusion: this is a CHEMICAL change. An acid (vinegar) and a base (baking soda) reacted together to form new substances, releasing gas bubbles as one of the clues — and it can't easily be undone.
Big picture: this links BOTH lessons together — acids and bases don't just sit there quietly; when they meet, they can react and create a genuine chemical change, with real evidence you now know how to check for.
