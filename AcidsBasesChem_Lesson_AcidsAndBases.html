Find the Flaw 🔍

Two students each wrote a plan for separating a tricky mixture. Your job: click the ONE thing wrong with the plan — or tell us if there's nothing wrong at all. This is a NEW kind of check, different from anything you've tried before on this topic.

Ploy's Plan — separating small plastic beads, sugar, and water, all mixed in one tub.

Step 1: Heat the whole tub until all the water evaporates away.
Step 2: Pick the dry plastic beads out of the leftover dry sugar by hand.
✔ Correct — Step 1 is the mistake. Evaporating first leaves the beads stuck in the dried sugar, forcing the messy hand-sort in Step 2. The beads should have been FILTERED out of the water first (they're insoluble), THEN the clear sugar water evaporated — no hand-sorting needed at all.
✘ Re-think it: evaporation only removes the liquid. What happens to the solid beads when the water disappears — do they vanish too, or are they still there mixed into the sugar?

Tam's Plan — separating sand, salt, and water, all mixed in one jar.

Step 1: Filter the mixture to catch the sand, leaving clear salt water behind.
Step 2: Evaporate the salt water to recover dry salt.
✔ Correct — Tam's plan has no mistake! Sand is insoluble, so filtering it out first is right; salt IS dissolved, so evaporating what's left is the only way to recover it. Order matters: filter the insoluble solid out BEFORE evaporating the truly dissolved one.
✘ Re-think it: check each step against what you know — is sand insoluble or dissolved? Is salt insoluble or dissolved? Does the order here match the filter-then-evaporate rule?

Why learn this? 🤔

Acids and bases are everywhere — the lemon in your tea, the vinegar in salad dressing, the soap on your hands, even the acid already inside your stomach. Knowing how to test which is which (safely!) is a real scientific skill, not just a fact to memorise.
📋 ASMOPSS connection: acid/base testing and everyday chemistry are recurring themes on real papers, but this program has never given them dedicated coverage before — this unit closes that gap directly.
Real life: gardeners in Thailand sometimes test their soil with a simple kit — soil that's too acidic or too basic (alkaline) can stop certain plants from growing well, so knowing how to test and fix it matters for real farming.
⚠️ Safety first: real scientists NEVER taste or touch unknown chemicals to test them — that's exactly why litmus paper exists: a safe way to test without tasting or touching anything dangerous.

What Makes Something an ACID? 🍋

An ACID is a substance that tastes sour (never taste-test unknowns!) and turns BLUE litmus paper RED.
Dip a strip of BLUE litmus paper into an acid, and the bottom half turns RED — that colour change is the proof, not the taste.

Everyday acids: lemon juice, vinegar, orange juice, and the acid already in your own stomach (Screen 3's warm-up!).

Key idea: litmus paper doesn't tell you HOW strong an acid is — just red or blue. A very mild acid and a very strong acid can both turn litmus red.

What Makes Something a BASE? 🧼

A BASE (also called ALKALINE) is a substance that feels slippery or soapy and turns RED litmus paper BLUE.
Three different results with the SAME two colours of litmus paper: a base turns red litmus blue, an acid turns blue litmus red, and something neutral changes neither strip at all.

Everyday bases: soap, baking soda, toothpaste — and stronger ones like oven cleaner (adults only, never for testing by hand).

Key idea: NEUTRAL substances (like pure water) are neither acid nor base — they don't change either colour of litmus paper at all.

Acid, Base, or Neutral? 🌳

Dip BOTH a red strip and a blue strip of litmus paper into the liquid. What happens?
↓
BLUE strip turns RED
It's an ACID (e.g. lemon juice, vinegar)
RED strip turns BLUE
It's a BASE (e.g. soap, baking soda)
NEITHER strip changes
It's NEUTRAL (e.g. pure water)
Key idea: you always need BOTH colours of litmus paper to be sure — using only a blue strip, you can spot an acid but can't tell a base apart from neutral (neither changes a blue strip).

Worked Example: Testing Three Liquids 🧪

Scenario: A student tests three unlabelled cups — Cup X, Cup Y, Cup Z — by dipping both colours of litmus paper into each.

Cup X: the blue strip turns red; the red strip stays red.
Cup Y: neither strip changes colour at all.
Cup Z: the red strip turns blue; the blue strip stays blue.

Step 1 — Cup X: a colour change happened, and it was blue→red. That's the acid signature. Cup X is an ACID.

Step 2 — Cup Y: no colour change at all, on either strip. That's the neutral signature. Cup Y is NEUTRAL.

Step 3 — Cup Z: a colour change happened, and it was red→blue. That's the base signature. Cup Z is a BASE.

Step 4 — could this have been done with only ONE colour of litmus paper? Only partly — a single blue strip could have caught Cup X (acid), but Cup Y and Cup Z would have looked identical (no change to a blue strip either way) — proving why BOTH colours are needed.
