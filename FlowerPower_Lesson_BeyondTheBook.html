Wind or Insect?

Farmers and gardeners need to know how a plant gets its pollen. It tells them which flowers need bees nearby and which do not.

Insect flowers advertise: bright petals and sweet scent. Wind flowers do not need to: they are small and dull, make lots of light pollen, and have feathery stigmas that catch pollen from the air.

Sort the plants by how they are pollinated.
Wind: small dull flowers, much light pollen, feathery stigmas. Insects: bright petals and sweet scent.

Self- or Cross-Pollination

Pollen can land on a stigma of the same flower: that is self-pollination. Or it can travel to a stigma on a different flower of the same kind: that is cross-pollination.

Self-pollination: same flower
Cross-pollination: to another flower
Pollination is the transfer of pollen from the anther to the stigma, on the same flower or on another one. Hibiscus and morning glory have both parts on one flower, so self-pollination can happen. Maize and cucumber have separate flowers, so pollen must travel between flowers. Insects and wind usually do cross-pollination. Cross-pollination mixes the parents, so it is useful.

Stamen and Pistil

Botanists give names to the two sets of parts. The stamen is the male part: the stalk and the anther with its pollen. The pistil is the female part: the stigma, with the ovary and its ovules below.

Stamen is the male part. Pistil is the female part.
Remember: the cell membrane controls what goes in and out of every cell, even a pollen grain. After the transfer of pollen from the anther to the stigma, the nucleus of the pollen grain fuses with the nucleus of the egg. Then the ovules develop into seeds. Hibiscus and morning glory, maize and cucumber: every one of them follows this path.

Real Size Under the Microscope

A microscope picture makes a pollen grain look big. Real size = picture size, then divide by the magnification. Watch one example, then do your own.

Pollen under a microscope

Watch me
Step 1. The picture shows a pollen grain 8 mm wide. The magnification is ×200. The picture is bigger than the real grain, so the real grain is smaller than 8 mm.
Step 2. To go from the picture back to the real size, divide by the magnification.
Step 3. 8 ÷ 200 = 0.04. The real pollen grain is 0.04 mm wide.
Step 4. Size check: 0.04 mm is smaller than 8 mm. It fits. A wrong move like 250 ÷ 20 = 12.5 divides without asking first whether the real size should be smaller.
