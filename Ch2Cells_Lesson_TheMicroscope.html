Quick Recap 🔁

From Lesson 1: the organisation ladder runs CELL → TISSUE → ORGAN → ORGAN SYSTEM → ORGANISM, and cells stay small because their surface can't keep up with supplying a growing volume.
Today: cells are tiny enough that nobody on Earth even knew they existed for almost all of human history. What changed that — and how do you actually use the instrument that changed it?

The Year Nobody Had Ever Seen a Cell 🕰️

Cells are far too small to see with the naked eye. For almost all of human history, nobody knew living things were built from them at all.
1665 — Robert Hooke: looking through an early microscope at a thin slice of cork (dead bark), Hooke saw tiny empty box-like compartments. They reminded him of the small bare rooms monks lived in — so he named them "cells," from the Latin word for "small room." The name stuck for over 350 years.
Soon after — Antonie van Leeuwenhoek: using microscopes he ground and polished himself, Leeuwenhoek was the first person to see living, moving, single-celled organisms swimming in pond water. He called these tiny moving creatures "animalcules" — today we'd call them microorganisms (Unit 4).
Key idea: Hooke and Leeuwenhoek didn't discover cells because they were smarter than everyone before them — they discovered cells because an INSTRUMENT finally let them see a level of reality that had always been there, just always invisible.

How a Microscope Works 🔬

Light travels UP through the microscope: light source → through the thin specimen → through the OBJECTIVE lens → through the EYEPIECE lens → into your eye.
Light travels UP from the light source, through the thin specimen on the stage, through the objective lens (which does most of the magnifying), up the body tube, and through the eyepiece lens into your eye. The focus knob moves the stage up and down to sharpen the image.
Key idea: the specimen MUST be thin enough for light to pass all the way through it — that's why you'll prepare wafer-thin slices, not thick chunks, whenever you use a real microscope.

Total Magnification 🧮

NEW numeric skill: TOTAL MAGNIFICATION = EYEPIECE magnification × OBJECTIVE magnification.
Worked example 1: a 10× eyepiece with a 4× objective gives 10 × 4 = 40× total magnification.
Worked example 2: the SAME 10× eyepiece, now with a 40× objective, gives 10 × 40 = 400× total magnification — ten times stronger, just by switching the objective lens.
Working BACKWARDS: if you need 100× total magnification and your eyepiece is 10×, what objective do you need? 100 ÷ 10 = a 10× objective.

The Trade-Off ⚖️

Higher magnification shows MORE detail but LESS of the specimen at once — and the image gets DIMMER too.
What's the problem right now?
↓
You can see the whole specimen, but no fine detail
GO UP — switch to a stronger objective
You've lost the specimen from view entirely
GO BACK DOWN and re-centre first — THEN go back up
Key idea: jumping straight to the highest magnification usually means you can't even find the specimen — it's easy to lose it once your field of view shrinks. Always find and centre it at LOW magnification first, then step up.

Why Specimens Are Stained 🎨

Quick preview: most cells are almost completely see-through (transparent) under a microscope, which makes their inner parts very hard to tell apart. Scientists add coloured STAINS to make different parts show up clearly — you'll prepare and stain a real slide in a future unit.

Trap-Recognition Drill #11 🚨

Watch for words like ALWAYS, NEVER, ONLY, and EVERY. A claim using one of these words is often too extreme to be true — even if it SOUNDS like the "stronger" or more impressive choice.

Drill. A student claims: "The highest magnification a microscope offers is ALWAYS the best one to use." Is this claim true?

✔ Correct — this is a false absolute claim (the word "ALWAYS" is the giveaway). The right magnification depends on the job: find and centre the specimen at LOW magnification first, then increase it.
✘ Re-think it: at the very highest magnification, is the field of view large enough to easily FIND a specimen you haven't centred yet?

1. In science class, a microscope is set up with a 10× eyepiece and a 40× objective lens attached. What total magnification does this give?

✔ Correct — total magnification = eyepiece × objective = 10 × 40 = 400×.
✘ Re-check Screen 5 — do you ADD the two numbers, or MULTIPLY them?

2. You need a total magnification of 150× and your eyepiece is 15×. Which objective lens do you need?

✔ Correct — 150 ÷ 15 = 10×. Working backwards, you divide the total by the eyepiece to find the objective.
✘ Re-check Screen 5's "working backwards" example — what operation undoes multiplication?

3. Why must a specimen be sliced very thin before viewing it under a microscope?

✔ Correct — a microscope works by shining light THROUGH the specimen, so it has to be thin enough to let that light pass.
✘ Re-check Screen 4 — which direction does light travel through the microscope, and what does it need to pass through?

4. You're looking through a microscope and have completely lost sight of your specimen at high magnification. What should you do?

✔ Correct — always re-find and re-centre a lost specimen at LOW magnification before stepping back up.

5. A classmate says: "You should always start on the HIGHEST magnification, since that gives you the most detail straight away." Evaluate this claim.

✔ Correct — this overgeneralizes. Start LOW to find and centre the specimen, since high magnification's narrow field of view makes it easy to lose.
