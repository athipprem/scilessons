Warm-up. An astronaut on a spacewalk bangs a hammer directly against the outside of their spaceship, out in empty space. Can a nearby astronaut, also outside in space, HEAR the bang?

✔ Correct — sound needs a medium (solid, liquid, or gas) to travel through. Space is a vacuum (no medium at all), so sound can't cross it.
✘ Re-think it: what does sound actually need in order to travel from place to place — and does outer space have that?

Warm-up. Sound can travel through solids, liquids, and gases. Through which of these three does sound travel FASTEST?

✔ Correct — sound travels fastest through solids (particles packed tightest together), then liquids, then slowest through gases (particles spread furthest apart).
✘ Re-think it: sound travels by particles bumping into their neighbours — which of the three states has its particles packed closest together?

Why learn this? 🤔

Your school class is starting Chapter 2 of your i-Science book: Cells. This unit matches it step by step, so what you learn here is exactly what you'll see in class and on your school tests.
Real life: every time you get a paper cut and it heals, or every time you grow a little taller this year, cell division is what's actually happening inside your body. Doctors, and even athletes recovering from an injury, rely on understanding this exact process.

Cells: The Building Blocks of Life 🧱

From Unit 14: every cell — plant or animal — has a nucleus (control centre), cytoplasm (jelly-like filling), and a cell membrane (thin boundary). Plant cells ALSO have a rigid cell wall, green chloroplasts, and a large vacuole — parts animal cells don't have.
NEW idea: EVERY living thing, from a single bacterium to a giant blue whale, is made of cells. Cells are the basic building blocks of ALL life.
Two ways living things are built from cells:
  • Unicellular — the whole body is just ONE cell (like bacteria)
  • Multicellular — the body is made of MANY cells working together (like a plant, an animal, or you)
Key idea: a human body is made of trillions of cells. But every single one of those trillions of cells started from far fewer cells — which brings us to today's new idea: how does a body get MORE cells?

Why Do Cells Divide? ➗

CELL DIVISION is when ONE cell splits into TWO new cells. Living things use cell division for two big reasons: GROWTH and REPAIR.
One cell divides to make two new cells — and both new cells are identical copies of the original.
GROWTH: a living thing gets bigger mainly because it makes MORE cells through division — NOT mainly because its existing cells swell up in size. A ten-year-old is taller than a baby chiefly because they have vastly more cells, not because each individual cell has grown huge.
REPAIR: when skin is cut or a bone is damaged, nearby cells divide to make new cells that replace the damaged or lost ones, healing the injury over time.
Key idea: cell division doesn't just happen once when you're a baby — it keeps happening throughout your ENTIRE life, for both growth (while you're still growing) and repair (whenever you're healing), even into adulthood.

Growth or Repair? 🌱🩹

Is the cell division making the body BIGGER, or fixing something DAMAGED?
↓
Making the body bigger
GROWTH — e.g. a child getting taller
Fixing something damaged
REPAIR — e.g. a scraped knee healing
Key idea: both growth and repair use the exact same underlying process — one cell dividing into two — the only difference is WHY the body needs those new cells at that moment.
