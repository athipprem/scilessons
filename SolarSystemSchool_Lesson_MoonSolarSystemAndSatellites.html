Quick Recap 🔁

From Lesson 1: the Sun is a star made of hot gases — the source of almost all Earth's energy. Earth has three layers (crust, mantle, core) and supports life thanks to the right temperature, an atmosphere, and liquid water.
From Unit 7: the Moon has no light of its own — it REFLECTS sunlight. It orbits Earth roughly every 27–29 days, and how much of its lit half we see from Earth is what causes phases.
Today: what does the Moon actually LOOK like up close, what other planets share the Solar System with Earth, and what are those objects people have launched into space?

The Moon 🌙

The Moon is Earth's only NATURAL satellite — a rocky, airless world covered in craters.
Key idea: the Moon has NO atmosphere and NO liquid water, so nothing can live there. With no air to blow it away, dust kicked up millions of years ago is often still sitting exactly where it landed.
Key idea — extreme temperatures: because the Moon has no atmosphere to trap heat or block the Sun, its daytime side can get scorching hot and its night side freezing cold — much more extreme than Earth, which its atmosphere helps keep more even.

The Solar System 🪐

The Solar System has the Sun at its centre, with 8 planets orbiting it — split into two families: rocky planets and gas giants.
Order from the Sun (closest to farthest): Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune. (Sizes and distances here are not to scale — just to show the order and grouping.)
Key idea — two planet families: the first 4 (Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars) are small, solid, and rocky — called ROCKY (terrestrial) planets. The next 4 (Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune) are huge and made mostly of gas — called GAS GIANTS. There's also a belt of rocky chunks called the ASTEROID BELT between Mars and Jupiter, marking the boundary between the two families.

Man-Made Satellites 📡

A SATELLITE is anything that orbits a bigger object. The Moon is a NATURAL satellite of Earth — but people have also built and launched thousands of ARTIFICIAL (man-made) satellites into orbit.
Key idea: "satellite" doesn't only mean the Moon! Any object circling Earth (or another planet) counts — natural ones like moons, and artificial ones built by people.
What man-made satellites are used for: weather satellites track storms and help forecast the weather; communication satellites relay TV, phone, and internet signals around the world; GPS (navigation) satellites help phones and cars figure out exactly where they are; research satellites study Earth, the Sun, or deep space.
Key idea — how they stay in orbit: a satellite is launched sideways at just the right speed. Earth's gravity constantly pulls it inward, but its sideways speed keeps carrying it forward — the balance between the two keeps it circling instead of falling to the ground OR flying off into space.

Trap-Recognition Drill #7 🚩

A tempting shortcut hides in what you just learned. Let's catch it before it catches you.

Warm-up. A student claims: "The Moon is the ONLY thing that orbits the Earth." Is this true?

✔ Correct — the Moon is Earth's only NATURAL satellite, but thousands of man-made (artificial) satellites also orbit Earth right now.
✘ Re-think it: the Moon is special because it's Earth's only NATURAL satellite — but "orbiting Earth" isn't limited to natural objects.
Key idea: "the Moon is Earth's only satellite" is a trap. It's Earth's only NATURAL satellite — there are thousands of ARTIFICIAL ones too.
