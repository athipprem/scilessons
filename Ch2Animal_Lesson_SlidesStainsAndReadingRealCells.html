Make a Real Slide

In Unit 48 you were promised you would one day prepare and stain a real slide. Today is the day. Scientists use slides to see cells that are far too small to see by eye, including the ones that make up your own body.
First, one thing to recall: the specimen must be very thin, so light can shine through it. A coverslip then goes on top, to flatten it and protect it.

Same Slide, as a List (Route B)

Same idea, now as steps with the reason for each.
  1. Scrape the inside of your cheek gently, with your own clean cotton bud. This gives a few thin cells.
  2. Smear it on the slide. A thin smear lets light shine through.
  3. Add one drop of methylene blue (iodine for plant cells). Almost everything in a cell is see-through, so the stain makes the nucleus stand out.
  4. Lower the coverslip at an angle, one edge first, using a toothpick. The liquid pushes the air out ahead of the glass, so no air bubbles get trapped.
  5. Look on low power first, then move up. You find the cells before you zoom in.

Two Stains, Two Jobs

Methylene blue is used for animal cells, like your cheek cells. It colours the nucleus blue.
Iodine is used for plant cells, like onion cells. It colours the nucleus yellow-brown.
Plant cell? Reach for the iodine. Animal cell? Reach for the methylene blue. A stain makes some parts stand out from the rest.

Cell or Air Bubble?

Rule: use at least two clues before you decide.
Air bubble clues: perfectly round, thick black rim, bright empty centre, no stained dot. A bubble also slides about if you press the coverslip. Cell clues: uneven outline, thin edge, a darker stained dot.

The surprise: onion cells have no chloroplasts. The bulb grows underground in the dark. So "no green" does not prove a cell is from an animal. That is why you need two clues. An onion slide stained with iodine shows thick walls but no green.
